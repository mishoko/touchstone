Your manager pays for their own bad trades.

A Uniswap v4 vault that checks every manager action against a deep market's price, and charges any shortfall to the manager's held-back pay.

Video walkthrough See it working, start to finish The quickest way to see what Touchstone does: the idea, the five scenes on real prices, and the live testnet transactions. Watch on YouTube ↗

You hand your money to a manager you cannot watch, and you pay them anyway. When a liquidity vault's manager moves your money, every trade has a cost: fees, a bad price, or a friend on the other side who profits. Today that cost comes out of your balance, and the manager's pay does not change.

Touchstone flips it. Every time the manager acts, the vault checks the result against the price on a much larger market. Any shortfall is taken from the manager's held-back pay and then from a bond the manager posted up front. If the manager cannot cover it, the action does not happen. Market moves are still yours.

Think of a broker whose every order is compared with the exchange's price, with any gap deducted from the broker's held-back bonus.

    How it works

    Checked against a price that is costly to fake.

    A pool on Uniswap is a shared pot of two tokens. Its spot price is cheap to push for a moment. So Touchstone never trusts it alone.

    Each vault is tied, for life, to a reference price: the 10-minute average of a much deeper pool. The manager may act only inside a narrow fence around it.

    When the manager acts, the vault values itself before and after, at both prices. The worse result is the execution cost. It is charged in full, from held-back pay first, then the bond. Two minutes later a markout checks the action again.

    The manager earns a base fee only for money placed in its range (measured in ticks). That pay is held back for weeks and vests only while the deep market's price is in range. Stay away too long and it is a breach.

    How costly? Holding the deep USDC/WETH pool's price 60 ticks off for one block costs about 1.04 WETH, measured on a mainnet copy (E8). The design assumes that reference is not moved; a single block builder controlling the whole two-minute window is outside it (Limits).

    Why two prices? The vault's own pool can be pushed cheaply, so pay and penalties follow the deep market's price. If only the vault's pool leaves the range, the fee pauses. If the deep market's price leaves it, vesting stops at once, and the fee and the 24-hour clock follow at the next hourly check.

    The numbers are the mainnet tier's (script/Tiers.sol). The testnet deployment runs the same rules faster (parameters under Builders).

    Who is involved

    Four parties. What each wants, and what the rules do.

    Depositor

    Wants

    Its share of the vault, no unmeasured execution costs taken from it, and a way out.

    The rules

    Withdraw in kind at any time, in every mode. A deposit followed by a withdrawal never profits. Deposits are refused for about two minutes until a pending check settles.

    Why

    So no rule, charge or removal can lock depositors in, and no one can time a deposit around a charge.

    Manager

    Wants

    Steady pay for keeping money working.

    The rules

    Paid only for money placed in range. Pays every measured shortfall. Loses held-back pay if absent.

    Why

    To make doing the job pay. In our simulations, with no outside party refreshing the reference price, it paid better than every gated lazy or lax strategy we tested (U-IC2). With one, two lax strategies came out ahead at 120% volatility.

    Arbitrageur

    Wants

    Profit from any price gap. (A trader who pulls an off-price pool back to the market.)

    The rules

    Trades freely and is never charged. A gap the manager creates becomes its profit, and the manager's charge.

    Why

    So a manager colluding with a trader pays the trader's gain itself.

    Anyone who pokes

    Wants

    A correct record, for example to trigger a breach or settle a charge.

    The rules

    Can call settle, poke, process a breach and refresh the reference. Cannot move funds.

    Why

    So the vault needs no keeper, operator, admin or off-chain feed.

    How the incentives line up. Every bit of cost the manager avoids is pay it keeps; gains never earn extra. Sloppy or self-dealing trades come out of its own pay and bond. Idling earns nothing. Walking away costs everything held back. In our simulations, with no outside party refreshing the reference price, doing the job paid better than every gated lazy or lax strategy we tested (U-IC2). With one, two lax strategies came out ahead at 120% volatility.

    Why a manager signs up. A record of its execution, kept by the pool, that anyone can check. A published term sheet. A fee for capital it keeps working.

        The deal

        Is 2.2% a good deal? Only if the manager does the job.

        The manager earns 2.2% a year on money it keeps working. It risks a small bond and pays its own bad trades. Pick a market and compare what each kind of manager takes home.

        Manager's net take-home in ETH over 180 days, for a 1,000 ETH vault: after charges and gas, from 400 simulated price paths per market, no third-party liquidity, gas at 5 gwei (U-IC2, no outside party refreshing the reference price). "Most it can earn" is the ceiling at a constant 1,000 ETH, fully in range: 2.2% a year × 1,000 ETH × 180 of 365 days (our arithmetic).

        For the manager

        A good deal when it does the job: re-places the range when the market moves, and keeps the money working. Lazy or idle strategies take home less. The margin is thinnest in wild markets. The 2.2% rate is fixed in the code and was not tuned after seeing the data; the 12-hour wait behind these bars was chosen after an earlier test failed (Limits).

        For depositors

        You pay 2.2% a year only on money that is placed and working, and only while both prices are in the range. Idle money costs nothing. Execution costs the vault measures are billed to the manager. Whether the trading fees your money earns beat 2.2% depends on trading volume, which these simulations do not include; we publish no break-even.

        The vault's value is a separate question. In the same simulations, the vault ended with more value under the lax strategies than under the manager that does the job (at 120%: about 558 and 569 against 314, from 1,000), because with no other traders in the model, time in range is mostly loss to arbitrage. Market moves stay with depositors (Limits).

        With an outside party refreshing the reference price, two lax strategies came out ahead at 120% volatility (Limits).

        Glossary

        Fifteen words, each built on the ones before it.

        Every underlined term on this page opens here. Hover a term to read it in place.

        Pool
        A shared pot of two tokens (here USDC, a dollar token, and ETH) that anyone can trade against. The mix in the pot sets the price.
        Spot price
        The pool's price right now, in the current block (about every 12 seconds on Ethereum).
        Vault and shares
        A joint account that puts depositors' tokens into a pool. Each depositor owns shares. The vault's total value (its NAV) divided by the shares is the price of one share.
        Manager (curator)
        The one party allowed to move the vault's money: where it sits and when it trades. The code calls it the curator.
        Range
        The price window in which the vault's money is placed in the pool and earns trading fees. Here it is about ±10% wide and must hold the price. Outside it, the money sits idle.
        Tick
        The pool's unit of price: one tick is a 0.01% step. 60 ticks is about 0.6%; the 2,000-tick range is about ±10%.
        Reference price
        The average price of the last 10 minutes on a separate, much larger pool, chosen once when the vault is created (a TWAP, time-weighted average price). It is expensive to push because that pool is deep.
        Fence
        A 60-tick band around the reference price. The manager may act only while the spot price is inside it. Outside, the action is refused.
        Execution cost (implementation shortfall)
        After each manager action, the vault prices its holdings before and after, at the spot price and at the reference price, and uses whichever makes the action look worst. The drop is the execution cost. A gain never earns the manager anything, and never offsets a loss.
        Base fee
        The manager's pay: 2.2% a year of the money actually placed in range, earned only while the spot price and the reference price are both inside the range.
        Deferred pay and vesting
        Pay is held back for four to five weeks (four to five epochs of 7 days) before it becomes the manager's to keep (it vests). It vests only while the reference price is in range.
        Bond
        Money the manager posts up front as first loss: 0.174% of the vault's value (1.74 ETH per 1,000; at least 1.74 ETH on mainnet settings). It is taken only after the deferred pay is used up.
        Charge
        The execution cost, taken in full from deferred pay, then from the bond. If the two cannot cover it plus a small reserve, the action is refused. A charge burns the manager's shares, so every other share is worth a little more.
        Markout (settlement)
        Prices on a blockchain lag by a block, so a manager could trade on a stale price. Two minutes later the vault checks the trade again against the deep market's price over those two minutes, and takes up to a further 0.06% of the vault's value from the manager if the trade looks worse. The manager cannot know that price when acting.
        Breach (a strike)
        24 hours of observed time with the reference price outside the range. A breach forfeits all deferred pay, burned for the benefit of every share and released over 7 days. Three breaches remove the manager.
